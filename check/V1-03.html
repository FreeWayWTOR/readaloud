THE READING: The Woman Who Carried the Vaccine
[1] On a cold morning in November 1803, a small ship called the María Pita left the Spanish port of A Coruña with one of the strangest cargoes in history. Below deck, twenty-two orphan boys, the youngest only three years old, were carrying a medicine that could save millions of lives, and they were carrying it inside their own arms. The only woman on board, a poor servant named Isabel Zendal, had promised to bring every one of them safely to the other side of the world.
[2] The medicine was the smallpox vaccine. Smallpox was the most feared disease of the time: it killed about three out of every ten people it infected and left many survivors blind or covered in scars. In 1796, the English doctor Edward Jenner had discovered that people who received a little material from a cowpox sore were protected. There was just one problem. The vaccine only stayed active for a few days, so when doctors tried to send it across the ocean on glass or dried threads, it almost always arrived dead.
[3] The Spanish doctor Francisco Xavier Balmis had an idea that was brilliant and, to many people, shocking. If the vaccine could not survive the journey in a bottle, it could survive in a human body. Every nine or ten days, two boys would be vaccinated with fresh material from the small sores on the arms of the boys before them, so that the vaccine passed from child to child like a flame from candle to candle. All he needed was a group of children who had never had smallpox, and someone to look after them.
[4] That someone was Isabel. She was the director of the orphanage of A Coruña, a single mother in a society that looked down on single mothers, and a woman who had worked as a servant most of her life. She was paid very little, but the children trusted her, and among the boys who sailed that November was her own son, Benito. For weeks at sea, she fed them, washed them, calmed them during storms and made sure that two of them always had the precious sores on their arms, because if the chain broke even once, the whole expedition would fail.
[5] It did not break. The María Pita stopped in Puerto Rico, Venezuela and Cuba, and in July 1804 it reached Veracruz. New Spain knew smallpox well: epidemics in 1779 and 1797 had killed thousands of people in Mexico City alone. As the expedition moved inland, doctors vaccinated thousands of people and created local boards to keep the work going after they left. In February 1805, Balmis, Isabel and twenty-six Mexican boys sailed from Acapulco to the Philippines, carrying the vaccine across a second ocean.
[6] When the mission was over, Isabel did something that surprised everyone: she did not go home. She returned to New Spain and settled in Puebla with her son, and there the documents lose her. Nobody knows when she died or where she is buried. Even her name became a puzzle, written in old papers as Zendal, Sendales or Cendala, as if history could not decide who she was.
[7] For almost two centuries, the doctors received the credit. Today, she is often described as the first nurse in history to take part in an international public health mission, and in 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic, a new hospital in Madrid was named after her. The army's operation to disinfect care homes was called Operation Balmis, but many Spaniards preferred to remember the woman who made his idea possible: a servant who crossed two oceans with twenty-two children and never lost one link of the chain.
