THE READING: The 47 Ronin
[1] On a freezing night in January 1703, forty-seven unemployed samurai are about to do something that will make them the most famous criminals in Japanese history. Snow has been falling over Edo since the early evening, and by midnight the streets are silent, which is exactly what the men moving toward the river want. They carry swords, spears, rope ladders and a heavy wooden hammer for breaking doors, and under their ordinary coats they are wearing armor they made in secret.
[2] To understand why they are here, we need to go back almost two years, to a spring morning in the shogun's castle. A young lord named Asano Naganori is preparing to welcome messengers from the emperor, an honor that comes with a long list of rules he barely understands. His teacher for the ceremony is Kira Yoshinaka, an older official who knows every rule and, according to many stories, expects a generous gift in return. Whether Kira insults Asano or Asano is simply exhausted and ill, nobody really knows. What the records tell us is that Asano suddenly pulls out his short sword in a castle corridor and attacks Kira.
[3] The cut on Kira's head is not serious, but the crime is. Drawing a sword inside the shogun's castle is so dangerous that Asano is ordered to perform seppuku, ritual suicide, before the sun goes down. His castle and lands are taken away, and his samurai, around three hundred men, lose their salaries, their homes and their master in a single day. They become ronin, "men of the waves," floating through life with no lord to serve. Kira, meanwhile, goes home without any punishment.
[4] Everyone in Edo expects Asano's men to take revenge, and Kira expects it too, which is why his spies follow their leader, Ōishi Kuranosuke, wherever he goes. Ōishi gives them a show. He moves to Kyoto, sends his wife away, and spends his nights drinking in the city's pleasure houses while people whisper that the great samurai has become a fool. It is a performance, and a very good one. Behind the drinking, Ōishi is writing letters, buying weapons and quietly deciding which of the ronin he can trust with his life.
[5] Now, on this snowy night, the waiting is over. The ronin divide into two groups and break through the front and back gates at the same moment, so that nobody inside can escape. They fight Kira's guards room by room, but when they reach his bedroom, it is empty, although the blankets are still warm. After a long search, they find the old man hiding in a small storage building near the kitchen. Ōishi offers him the chance to die with honor. Kira refuses to answer, and the ronin kill him.
[6] As the sun comes up, the forty-seven walk across the city to Sengaku-ji, the temple where Asano is buried, carrying Kira's head. Crowds come out to watch them pass, and by the time they reach the grave, the whole city is talking about them. They wash the head in the temple well, place it in front of their lord's stone, and then they do something unexpected: they wait for the government to decide what to do with them.
[7] It is not an easy decision. The ronin have broken the law, but they have also shown the kind of loyalty every samurai is supposed to admire. After weeks of debate, the shogun allows them to die with honor rather than as common criminals, and forty-six of them perform seppuku. The forty-seventh, Terasaka Kichiemon, is missing. Some say Ōishi sent him home to report the news; others say he ran. He lives to about eighty-seven.
[8] Today, the graves of the ronin stand in neat rows beside their lord's, and visitors still leave incense there every December. Their story has become plays, films and books, but more than three hundred years later, one question remains: why did Asano draw his sword?
