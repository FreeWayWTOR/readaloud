THE READING: The Lady Who Fought Smallpox
[1] In April 1721, in a house in London, a three-year-old girl sat on her mother's lap while a surgeon made a tiny cut in her arm. Several of the King's doctors stood around the room, watching carefully. They were not there to help. They were there because they expected the experiment to go wrong, and if the little girl became seriously ill, they wanted to be witnesses. Her mother, Lady Mary Wortley Montagu, did not look worried at all.
[2] To understand her confidence, you need to know how much she hated smallpox. It was the most feared disease in Europe, killing about 400,000 people every year and leaving many survivors blind or with deep scars on their faces. It had already taken her only brother, and in 1715 it almost took her. She survived, but the disease destroyed her famous beauty and left her without eyelashes, which was a terrible loss for a woman whose life at court depended on her appearance.
[3] Lady Mary had never been good at following rules. As a girl, when women were not allowed to study at university, she taught herself Latin in secret in her father's library. When her father chose a husband for her, she ran away and married the man she loved instead. So when her husband became the British ambassador to the Ottoman Empire and the family moved to Constantinople, today's Istanbul, she did not stay inside the embassy. She went out, visited women's baths and homes, and wrote long letters about everything she saw.
[4] In one of those letters, she described something that seemed impossible. Every autumn, old women went from house to house with a nutshell full of material from a smallpox blister. They made a small scratch in a person's skin and put a little of it inside. A few days later, the person got a mild form of the disease, recovered quickly and was protected for life. People called it engrafting. In 1718, Lady Mary asked the embassy surgeon to do it to her five-year-old son. He had a light fever, and then he was fine.
[5] Back in England, nobody wanted to hear about it. For many doctors, a medical idea that came from uneducated foreign women, and was now being promoted by an English lady with no training, was not medicine at all. But in 1721, a terrible outbreak of smallpox hit London, and Lady Mary decided that words were not enough. That was why her daughter was sitting on her lap with the King's doctors in the room. The girl had a few spots and recovered completely.
[6] The demonstration convinced someone very important: Caroline, the Princess of Wales, who had two young daughters of her own. Before risking them, the royal family wanted more proof, so in August 1721 six prisoners who had been sentenced to death were offered a deal. If they agreed to be inoculated and survived, they would go free. All six survived. The next year, the princess had her daughters inoculated, and suddenly the risky foreign trick became fashionable.
[7] Inoculation was not perfect. Of the 897 people inoculated in England between 1721 and 1728, seventeen died, but that was far better than the three in ten who died when they caught smallpox naturally. Slowly, the method spread around the world, and in 1797, during an epidemic in New Spain, people were inoculated in Jonacatepec, very close to us in Morelos. By then, an English doctor named Edward Jenner was about to create something even safer: the vaccine.
[8] Lady Mary did not live to see it, but she prepared the way. When the World Health Organization declared in 1980 that smallpox had disappeared from the planet, the story began, in a way, with a woman who refused to accept that her children's lives were simply a matter of luck.
