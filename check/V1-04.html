THE READING: The Young Woman Who Found Sea Monsters
[1] In the winter of 1811, on a cold, windy beach in the south of England, a twelve-year-old girl was staring at a rock. Her brother had found a strange skull in the cliffs a few months before, a long head with enormous eyes and a mouth full of sharp teeth, and now Mary Anning was looking for the rest of the creature. When she finally found it, it took her and a team of workmen months to dig it out. The skeleton was more than five meters long, and the people of the little town of Lyme Regis had only one word for it: monster.
[2] The Annings were poor, and fossils were how they survived. Mary's father, a carpenter, had taught his children to search the beach for "curiosities," strange stones shaped like snakes or fingers, which they sold to tourists on a small table outside their house. When he died in 1810, he left his family nothing but debts. From then on, Mary, her brother Joseph and their mother lived on what the sea gave them, and the best time to look was after a storm, when pieces of the cliff fell onto the beach. It was dangerous work. In 1833, a landslide killed Mary's dog, Tray, just a few meters away from her.
[3] The "monster" was an ichthyosaur, a sea reptile that had lived about 200 million years earlier, and it arrived at a very uncomfortable moment for science. At that time, most people in Europe believed that the Earth was only a few thousand years old and that God would never allow a species to disappear completely. A creature like nothing alive anywhere in the world was a difficult problem. Perhaps, some scientists began to think, animals could become extinct after all.
[4] Mary was not just lucky. She learned to read at a free church school for poor children, and then she taught herself geology and anatomy, cutting up modern fish and sea animals to understand how the ancient ones had worked. Visitors were often surprised that a poor young woman with almost no education knew more about fossils than the gentlemen scientists who came to buy them. One of them wrote that she understood the subject better than anyone else in the kingdom.
[5] In 1823, Mary made her most spectacular discovery: the first complete skeleton of a plesiosaur, a sea reptile with a tiny head and a neck so long that it looked like a snake pulled through the body of a turtle. It looked so strange that the most famous scientist in Europe, Georges Cuvier of France, suggested that it was a fake. The Geological Society of London held a special meeting to examine the proof, and in the end Cuvier admitted his mistake.
[6] Mary was not at that meeting. She could not attend, because women were not allowed to be members of the Society, and they would not be accepted until 1904. Again and again, scientists bought her fossils, wrote about them and became famous, while her name appeared nowhere in their articles. "The world has used me so unkindly," she wrote in a letter. Her life remained hard, and in 1847 she died of cancer at the age of forty-seven.
[7] Her ideas lived on. Her discoveries helped scientists accept that the Earth was millions of years old and that whole groups of animals had disappeared, ideas that later prepared the way for Charles Darwin. In 2010, the Royal Society named her one of the ten British women who had most influenced the history of science, and since 2022 a bronze statue of Mary and her dog has stood in Lyme Regis, looking out at the cliffs where she found her monsters.
[8] Fame came too late for Mary, but there is a popular tongue twister that some people believe was inspired by her: "She sells seashells on the seashore." Whether or not that is true, the little girl who sold "curiosities" on a table outside her house had changed the way we understand the history of life.
