THE READING: The Doctor Nobody Believed
[1] In the 1840s, the women of Vienna had a terrible secret rule: if you had to go to the city's free hospital to have your baby, you prayed that it was not a day for the First Clinic. Some women begged on their knees to be sent anywhere else. Others preferred to give birth in the street on the way to the hospital. Everyone knew that in the First Clinic, mothers died, and nobody, not even the doctors, could explain why.
[2] The killer was a disease called childbed fever, and in some months it killed up to three in ten new mothers in that clinic. The strange thing was that the Second Clinic, in the same hospital, had far fewer deaths. The only real difference between them was the staff. In the First Clinic, doctors and medical students helped the mothers. In the Second, midwives did. Most doctors had an easy explanation: the problem was "miasma," a kind of bad air that smelled of rotten things. Germs had not been discovered yet.
[3] In 1846, a young Hungarian doctor named Ignaz Semmelweis started working in the First Clinic, and the deaths became his obsession. He tested every idea he could think of. Was it the position of the women in bed? He changed it. Was it the priest who walked through the ward ringing a bell when a mother was dying, frightening the others? He asked him to take another route. Nothing changed. The women kept dying, and the women who gave birth in the street, strangely, almost never got sick.
[4] The answer came through a tragedy. In 1847, his friend Jakob Kolletschka, a professor, was accidentally cut by a student's knife while they were examining a body. A few days later, he was dead, and his symptoms were exactly the same as the mothers'. Semmelweis suddenly understood. Every morning, the doctors and students examined corpses in the autopsy room, and then they walked directly to the maternity ward to examine women in labor, without washing their hands. In those days, surgeons were proud of their dirty coats, because the blood showed how much experience they had.
[5] In May 1847, Semmelweis ordered everyone to wash their hands in a chlorine solution before touching a patient. The smell was awful and the students complained, but the results were extraordinary. In the following months, deaths in the First Clinic fell from more than ten percent to around one or two percent. He had found the answer to one of the biggest medical mysteries of his time, and he had the numbers to prove it.
[6] Then something unbelievable happened: almost nobody listened. Many doctors were insulted by the idea that a gentleman's hands could carry death, and Semmelweis could not explain exactly what the invisible "particles" on their hands were. He was also not good at persuading people. Instead of publishing his evidence calmly, he wrote angry letters calling his critics murderers. In 1849, the hospital did not renew his contract, and he returned to Hungary, more and more frustrated and isolated.
[7] His story has a sad ending. In 1865, he was taken to a mental hospital, possibly with an illness of the brain, and he died there two weeks later, aged forty-seven. The cause was an infected wound, very probably the same kind of infection he had fought all his life. Only a few years later, Louis Pasteur proved that germs cause disease, and the surgeon Joseph Lister began to use antiseptics in operations. Suddenly, the whole world understood what Semmelweis had been trying to say.
[8] Today he is called "the savior of mothers," and psychologists even use his name for a human habit: the "Semmelweis reflex," our tendency to reject new evidence when it goes against what we already believe. So the next time you wash your hands, remember the doctor who was right too early.
